# scripture-kitchen

Bible translators keep their work in USFM: plain-text files, one book per file, with backslash markers like `\v 16` for verses and `\f` for footnotes. scripture-kitchen is the engine that reads those files without losing a byte. Its main user is Sefer, a translation editor that runs in the browser and calls the engine through WebAssembly, often on every keystroke. The engine checks the markup, finds chapters and verses, and hands the editor compact buffers of numbers. A second product, Sous Chef, learns what a translation normally does and flags what breaks its own pattern. It shows plain counts ("this spelling occurs 1 of 1,053 times") and leaves the call to a person. Everything is Rust; the editor gets one JavaScript package.

## The pieces

One Rust workspace, two products, one JS package.

- **[[onion-engine|Onion]]** reads USFM losslessly: [[scanner]] to [[token]]s, tokens to the [[cst]], and everything else derived from those.
- **[[sous-chef|Sous Chef]]** finds reviewable inconsistencies from counts alone, and never depends on onion.
- **[[galley]]** is the one crate allowed to see both. It caches ([[pantry]]), publishes ([[expediter]]) and ships the [[galley-package]] that [[sefer]] runs.

## Where to start

Read the [[cst]]. It is the base nearly everything reads: [[mask]], [[lint]], the [[exports]] and the [[dish]]. Then [[toc]] (where things are, no tree needed), then [[chunk]] and the [[chunk-cache]] (how a keystroke avoids re-reading a book).

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  source --> scanner --> token --> cst
  token --> toc
  cst --> mask & lint & exports
  cst & lint & toc --> plate --> dish --> sefer
  mask & toc --> onion-book --> sous-core --> findings-snapshot --> sefer
```
